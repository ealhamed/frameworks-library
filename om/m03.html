The Physics & Psychology of Waiting

Beau Ties case · four waiting-line truths · Maister's psychology · the newsvendor.

Idea Diagram Frameworks Apply Reading

The core idea

Queues obey factory physics: waits explode as utilisation approaches 100%, and variability makes everything worse at every level. But customers experience waits psychologically — occupied, explained, fair waits feel shorter than they are. The cheapest capacity decision is often not another server but a managed perception, and the staffing question is always a cost trade-off, never a target picked from air. — after the CD59 Module 3 material

Put simply · Why the queue feels worse than it is.

Two queues, same ten-minute wait. In one you stand in silence with no idea when your turn comes. In the other a screen says "9 minutes", someone hands you a menu, and the line visibly moves. The second wait feels half as long. Nothing about the service changed.

Underneath, queues follow physics: run a system near full capacity and waits do not grow gently, they explode. And the more unpredictable the arrivals, the worse it gets at every load level.

So the playbook has two moves: make the wait feel fair and occupied (cheap), and keep a little slack or flexible helpers for the spikes (worth it). Firms that skip the first move pay for the second twice.

The hero diagram

Where waits come from.

Two dials govern every queue: how loaded the system is, and how variable arrivals and service are. The dangerous cell is the one most operations drift into while chasing efficiency.

Frameworks in this module

Named ideas to remember.

Four Waiting-Line Truths · CD59 Module 3
Higher utilisation, longer waits · The same increase hurts more from a higher base · More variability, worse waits at any load · More capacity flexibility, shorter waits
The curves differ by industry; the shapes never do. Any staffing argument that ignores the non-linearity near full utilisation is wrong before it starts.
The Psychology of Waiting · Maister, 1985
Unoccupied and uncertain waits feel longer · Unexplained and unfair waits feel longer · Anxiety stretches time; company shrinks it
Perceived waits are manageable at near-zero cost — entertain, explain, and make the queue visibly fair before you buy a single extra server.
Queueing Approximation · CD59 Module 3
Inputs: utilisation U, servers M, variability CVa and CVs · Output: average queue length Lq, hence wait via Little's Law · Good enough for staffing decisions
You do not need simulation to size a call centre — the approximation formula turns a target wait into a number of servers you can defend.
The Newsvendor Model · single-period ordering canon
Order once, demand uncertain · Overage cost vs underage cost · Optimal service level balances the two
When you can only order once, the question is never "what will demand be?" but "which mistake is cheaper?" — and the answer sets your order.

How to apply

Fixing a wait people complain about.

  1. Split actual from perceived. Measure the real wait, then walk the queue as a customer. The complaint is usually about the experience, not the minutes.
  2. Deploy Maister first. Occupy the wait, explain it, guarantee its fairness. This costs almost nothing and often ends the complaint.
  3. Then do the physics. Compute utilisation and variability. If U is above ~85% at peak, no amount of psychology will save you.
  4. Buy flexibility before capacity. Cross-trained staff who flex to the queue beat a permanent extra server on cost, because they absorb variability.
  5. Frame staffing as a trade-off. Cost of waiting vs cost of servers, newsvendor-style. A 1-minute target is a choice with a price tag, not a virtue.
