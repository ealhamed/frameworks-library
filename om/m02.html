Little's Law & the Bottleneck

Ocean Cove case · I = R × T · capacity = min of the stages · KPIs meet strategy.

Idea Diagram Frameworks Apply

The core idea

A restaurant at lunch, a clinic, a visa office: each is a chain of stages, and the chain's capacity is the minimum of its stages' capacities. Around that hard truth sits a soft one — the KPIs a firm tracks either serve its strategic position or quietly fight it. Little's Law ties the visible symptom (a crowd) to the operational causes (arrival rate, time in system) with no statistics required. — after the CD59 Module 2 material

Put simply · Counting people in the queue tells you the wait.

Stand outside a coffee shop. Twelve people inside, and the barista hands over four cups a minute. You now know the wait is three minutes — without a stopwatch, without asking anyone. That is Little's Law: how many, how fast, how long — know two and you get the third.

The second trick is knowing where the queue really comes from. The counter looks busy, but if the espresso machine can only do three cups a minute, the machine is the bottleneck and hiring another barista changes nothing.

Firms make both mistakes at scale: they never count the crowd, and they expand the visible stage instead of the binding one.

The hero diagram

Little's Law, the process physicist's identity.

Inventory equals throughput times time-in-system. Twenty orders waiting at a kitchen that clears two a minute means a ten-minute wait — always, on average, regardless of the distribution. Know two, get the third.

Frameworks in this module

Named ideas to remember.

Little's Law · Little, 1961
I: units in the system · R: throughput rate · T: time in system
An identity, not a model — it holds for any stable process, which makes it the fastest sanity check in operations: measure two quantities, infer the one you cannot see.
The Bottleneck Rule · CD59 Module 2 · Ocean Cove
Stage capacity = realistic rate per stage · System capacity = min{kitchen, waiters, tables} · Adding capacity off-bottleneck adds nothing
Before recommending more seats, check whether the dining room is actually the binding stage. At Ocean Cove's lunch, it usually is not.
Operations-Strategy Fit · CD59 Module 2
Strategic position: who you serve, on what promise · Operational configuration: what your process is good at · KPIs: what behaviour you are actually buying
Critique a KPI by asking which position it serves. A speed metric in a firm selling intimacy is a strategy leak, not a dashboard detail.
Utilisation & Congestion · airline scheduling illustration
High utilisation = an asset rarely idle · Rarely idle = no slack to absorb disruption · Delays propagate through the day
The utilisation your CFO loves and the delays your customers hate are the same number seen from two sides.

How to apply

Reading a congested operation without a simulation.

  1. Count the crowd. I, the number in the system, is usually visible. It is the cheapest measurement you will ever take.
  2. Divide by throughput. T = I/R turns the crowd into a wait time. If the answer embarrasses the service promise, you have your headline.
  3. Locate the binding stage. Compute per-stage capacity and take the minimum. Intuition reliably picks the most visible stage, not the slowest one.
  4. Interrogate the KPIs. List what is measured. Ask which strategic position each metric serves. Expect at least one that serves none.
  5. Price the slack. Before pushing utilisation higher, name what the remaining idle time is currently buying you: resilience, quality, or recovery speed.
