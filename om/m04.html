The Bullwhip & the Shared Ledger

potato game · W'Up Bottlery & Barilla · bullwhip causes · MTS/MTO, reorder policies.

Idea Diagram Frameworks Apply

The core idea

Play four stages of a supply chain with steady end-demand and watch orders at the factory swing wildly: the bullwhip is produced by the players' own rational behaviour. Each stage forecasts from its neighbour's orders rather than real demand, batches, hedges against shortages — and amplifies. The fixes share one principle: let one decision-maker see one truth, from vendor-managed inventory to simply sharing the sell-through data. — after Lee, Padmanabhan & Whang

Put simply · Whisper down the supply chain.

Play the whisper game with numbers. The shop sells 100 units like clockwork, but rounds its order up "to be safe". The depot sees a bigger order, assumes demand is rising, and adds its own cushion. By the factory, a flat 100 has become an order for 400 — followed next month by zero.

Nobody was foolish. Everyone reacted sensibly to the only number they could see: the order in front of them. That is the bullwhip — the chain amplifies its own echo.

The cure is embarrassingly simple and organisationally hard: show everyone the till data, or let one party manage the whole shelf. The pasta company Barilla fought this battle in the 1990s; the potato game makes you fight it yourself.

Frameworks in this module

Named ideas to remember.

The Bullwhip Effect · Lee, Padmanabhan & Whang, 1997
Demand-signal processing: forecasting from orders, not demand · Order batching · Price fluctuations and forward buying · Shortage gaming
The whip is endogenous — four rational local behaviours produce collective madness, so exhorting people to forecast better cannot fix it.
Vendor-Managed Inventory · Barilla & W'Up Bottlery cases
Supplier sees true sell-through · Supplier decides replenishment · Customer sets service-level guardrails
Move the ordering decision to whoever has the best information. Barilla's distributors resisted giving up control — the selling of VMI matters as much as the maths.
Inventory Policy Menu · CD59 Module 4
Strategic: make-to-stock vs make-to-order · Continuous review: reorder point R, quantity Q · Periodic review: order-up-to level · Safety stock against lead-time demand
Pick the policy before arguing the numbers: MTS buys speed with inventory risk, MTO buys certainty with waiting, and the review policy decides who checks what, when.

How to apply

Diagnosing amplification in a chain you sit inside.

  1. Plot demand vs your orders. If your order variance exceeds your demand variance, you are the whip — before blaming anyone upstream.
  2. Name which of the four causes you commit. Batching for freight, hedging orders in shortages, buying ahead of promotions: all locally sensible, all amplifying.
  3. Share sell-through, not orders. The cheapest intervention is letting upstream see real demand. Information beats inventory.
  4. Consider moving the decision. VMI works when the supplier has better visibility and the customer keeps guardrails. Expect political resistance to exceed technical difficulty.
  5. Match policy to product. Steady runners: continuous review with safety stock. Lumpy or seasonal: periodic order-up-to. One-shot: newsvendor.
