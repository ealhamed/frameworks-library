m.01 · I · Measure · Measurement

GDP, Three Ways

after Kuznets · expenditure, income, value added · one flow, three lenses, known blind spots.

Idea Diagram Frameworks Apply Reading

The core idea

Everything an economy produces can be counted three equivalent ways: what buyers spend, what workers and owners earn, and the value each producer adds. The result is GDP, and per head at purchasing power parity it is the working proxy for living standards. It measures market production, not welfare: free goods, home production, pollution and inequality all sit outside the number, by construction. — after Kuznets and the national accountants

Put simply · Judging a family by its shopping receipts.

GDP adds up everything a country buys and sells. That is like judging a household by its receipts: it captures a lot, and you can compare this month to last month once you strip out price rises.

But receipts miss the meals cooked at home, the free games the kids play, the smoke the neighbouring factory pumps into the garden, and whether one person keeps all the pocket money.

So economists use the receipts — carefully. Per person, inflation-adjusted, price-level-adjusted, and with a list of what the receipts can never show.

Frameworks in this module

Named ideas to remember.

Three Approaches to GDP · national accounting tradition
Expenditure: C + I + G + NX · Income: labour income + capital income · Value added: summed over every producer
Spending is income is value added — if your three answers differ, you double-counted an intermediate good.
Real GDP & PPP · Penn World Table tradition
Nominal vs real (base-year prices) · Purchasing power parity across countries · GDP per capita as the standard proxy
Correct for inflation over time and price levels across borders — India is $2.7T at market rates and $10.5T at PPP.
GDP's Blind Spots · CD56 Module 1
Consumer surplus (free digital goods) · Non-market and underground activity · Externalities priced at zero · Distribution invisible in a total
GDP counts what is priced. Any welfare conclusion needs at least one measure it does not contain.
Models over Data · CD56 Module 1
Correlation is not causality · Counterfactuals are never observed · Deliberate simplification as a feature
Policy questions are counterfactual questions, and only a model can hold the rest of the world fixed.

How to apply

When someone quotes a GDP number at you.

  1. Ask real or nominal. A growth figure that includes inflation is a price story wearing a production costume.
  2. Ask market rates or PPP. Cross-country league tables flip depending on the answer — India is 3% of world GDP one way and 8% the other.
  3. Divide by people. Totals flatter big countries. Living standards are always per capita.
  4. Name what the number misses. Free apps, home work, pollution, who actually gets the income. If the argument depends on one of these, GDP cannot settle it.
  5. Separate measurement from causation. The data says what happened. Why it happened, and what a policy would change, needs a model.

Key reading · CD56 Module 1 · digital module

From measurement to theory.

The module's quiet centrepiece is a ranking exercise: order ten countries by happiness knowing only GDP per capita. Israel, at $46K, outranks Singapore at $133K — the correlation between income and well-being is steep for poor countries and weak for rich ones. One estimate puts WhatsApp's uncounted consumer surplus near 1% of Dutch GDP. GDP remains the best single number we have; the module's point is to know exactly what kind of number it is.

GDP is a measure of production wearing a welfare costume. Useful — as long as nobody forgets the costume.
