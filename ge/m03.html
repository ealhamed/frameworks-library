m.03 · II · Why nations are rich · Productivity

TFP & the Long Shadow

TFP = technology × efficiency · institutions decide what your machines are worth.

Idea Diagram Frameworks Apply Reading

The core idea

Rich countries are not just better equipped; they use what they have better. That residual — total factor productivity — bundles the technology frontier with the efficiency of everything around it: courts, schools, finance, stability. Institutions are causal, not decorative: Korea split in 1950 and diverged tenfold on the same peninsula. And because ideas are non-rival, technology is the one input that escapes diminishing returns. — after the CD56 Module 3 material

Put simply · Two kitchens with the same oven.

Give two restaurants identical ovens and identical staff numbers. One turns out twice the meals. The difference is not equipment — it is recipes, organisation, trust between the cooks, and whether the head chef promotes the talented or the loyal.

Countries work the same way. Korea split into two kitchens in 1950 with the same people and the same weather. The one with better rules now produces ten times more per person.

And unlike ovens, recipes can be copied by everyone at once without wearing out. That is why ideas — not machines — are the only engine of growth that never runs out of road. The question this module leaves open is whether we are finding fewer good recipes than we used to.

The hero diagram

What hides inside the residual.

TFP is measured as what is left over after capital and labour — but it is not a mystery. Each satellite is a channel that raises or wastes the output of the same machines and people.

Frameworks in this module

Named ideas to remember.

TFP: Frontier and Catch-up · CD56 Module 3
Technology: pushing the frontier out · Efficiency: operating closer to it · Low TFP = inferior tech OR wasted resources
A country can grow by inventing or by fixing how it uses what already exists — most countries have more room in the second.
Institutions as Deep Cause · institutional economics · North & South Korea as the experiment
Rule of law, regulation, finance, social insurance · Natural experiments over correlations · Aid without institutions gets consumed
Same people, same geography, different rules: a tenfold income gap in two generations. Machines pay off only inside good institutions.
Macro & Micro Inventions · Mokyr · Lever of Riches
GPTs: radical, rare, ab nihilo · Micro inventions: frequent refinements · Slow diffusion — decades, not quarters
Micro-refinements deliver most productivity; occasional GPTs reset the ceiling. Even electricity took forty years to show up in the numbers.
Displacement & Reinstatement · Acemoglu & Restrepo, 2019 · Autor, 2015
Displacement: machines take over tasks · Reinstatement: new tasks where labour keeps the edge · Polanyi's paradox: tacit skills resist automation
Automation is a race between the two effects. History has reinstated, which is why there are still so many jobs, but nothing guarantees the balance.
The Secular Stagnation Debate · Hansen 1938 · Rachel & Summers 2019 · Gordon vs the optimists
Symptoms: slowing growth, falling real rates · Gordon's six headwinds + Philippon's lost competition · Rebuttals: mismeasurement, J-curve diffusion
Hold both cases: R&D returns are falling (Bloom et al), but every GPT looked disappointing in its first decades.

How to apply

Diagnosing an economy (or a firm) that under-produces.

  1. Split the gap: equipment or usage? If peers get more from the same inputs, your problem is TFP, and buying more inputs will not fix it.
  2. Audit the institutions. Contract enforcement, talent allocation, credit access, political stability — each one is a multiplier on everything else.
  3. Respect the long shadow. History constrains the menu: partitioned regions and extractive legacies do not reset because a plan says so.
  4. Fund ideas differently from machines. Ideas are non-rival and escape diminishing returns — R&D incentives matter most for economies near their steady state.
  5. Time technology soberly. Expect the J-curve: real gains from a GPT arrive after years of reorganisation, not in the quarter you adopt it.

Key reading · Michalopoulos & Papaioannou · American Economic Review

The Long-Run Effects of the Scramble for Africa.

Between 1870 and 1914, European control of Africa went from 10% to nearly 90%, with borders drawn in ignorance of what they cut through. Michalopoulos and Papaioannou — the latter LBS faculty — show the partition of ethnic homelands was as-good-as-random, then trace its consequences: partitioned ethnicities suffer more political violence and are poorer and less educated today. It is the course's cleanest demonstration that history is a causal force, not background colour.

Lines drawn on a map in 1885 still predict who is poor in 2025. That is what a long shadow means.
